Managing Open-Weight Model Risk

Enterprises keep asking whether open-weight models are “safe.” Microsoft and NVIDIA’s answer: that’s the wrong question. Openness changes who holds the controls — not how risky the system is. Risk lives in the use case, the deployment, and the safeguards wrapped around the model.

The idea in five lines

  • Openness isn’t the risk — an open-weight model’s risk comes from its use case, deployment, and safeguards, not from whether the weights are public.
  • Judge the system, not the model — the same model is low-risk in an internal drafting tool and high-risk in a customer-facing agent wired to data and tools.
  • A shared risk language — Microsoft and NVIDIA’s new whitepaper offers five categories to align engineering, security, legal, and business teams.
  • Evaluations are your evidence — map each risk to concrete evals; Microsoft Foundry adds groundedness and safety scoring, automated red teaming, and content-safety controls.
  • Tie every risk to a control — layer safeguards across model, access, input/output filtering, monitoring, and human review, and revisit them as things change.
So what for you

Before you approve an open-weight model for anything beyond a sandbox, write down what it is actually wired to — the data, the tools, the users — and attach each risk to a named control and an accountable owner. “It’s open-weight” is not a risk assessment.

The reframe

Weight openness is not a risk rating

The instinct is to sort models into “open” and “closed” and treat one bucket as safer. Microsoft argues that misses where risk actually comes from: the model, the use case, the deployment, and the safeguards around it. The useful questions are what the model is used for, what could go wrong in that specific context, and what evidence and controls you have to show it won’t.

The same model carries very different risk in an internal drafting tool than in a customer-facing workflow connected to external data and tools.

Evaluating the model on its own doesn’t establish the safety of the agent or application built on it. You also have to assess the permissions it holds, the data it can reach, the tools it can call, the runtime it runs in, and the operational controls around it. The model is one layer of the risk picture, not the whole of it.

What shipped

A shared language for the whole org

Microsoft and NVIDIA published a joint whitepaper, Risk Management for Open-Weight Models in Enterprise — the first in a planned series — built on ISO standards and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework. Its core contribution is a common taxonomy so that engineering, security, legal, safety, and business teams can describe the same risk in the same words instead of talking past each other.

Importantly, the taxonomy is not a policy or a deployment checklist. The guidance is to adapt it to your environment and, for each risk you care about, link it to the evaluations that measure it, the mitigations that reduce it, and the person accountable for it.

The taxonomy

Five categories to name the risk

01

Cybersecurity

Exploitation, abuse, and the model becoming an attack surface or tool.

02

Data protection

Leakage, memorization, and mishandling of sensitive or regulated data.

03

Content

Harmful, unsafe, or policy-violating outputs the model can generate.

04

Human–AI interaction

Over-reliance, manipulation, and unsafe patterns of use by people.

05

Bias

Unfair or skewed behavior across groups, inputs, and contexts.

Adapt the categories to your environment — then link each risk to an evaluation, a mitigation, and an accountable owner.

The paper focuses mainly on model-layer risks; future joint work is slated to extend the same approach to the runtime, the harness, and the application layers.

Evidence & controls

Evaluations as evidence, controls at every layer

A taxonomy only helps if each named risk is backed by evidence. The whitepaper maps evaluations to a selection of risks across the five categories. Microsoft Foundry is named as one way to produce that evidence: built-in and custom quality, groundedness, and safety evaluations; automated red teaming; and configurable content-safety controls that help detect and mitigate harmful inputs and outputs, including prompt attacks. Microsoft’s own caveat: validate which controls are actually available for your model and deployment — including what is still in preview — and combine them with application-specific testing, monitoring, and human oversight.

Control

Open means more choices

Open-weight access gives you more control over evaluation, modification, and deployment — but model access and deployment are separate decisions, since open-weight models can also run on managed platforms.

Responsibility

Depends on how you run it

A self-operated deployment may take on safeguards a managed service would otherwise provide. Who owns which control shifts with the service and the configuration you choose.

Layers

Safeguards stack

Controls can sit at model changes, access controls, input/output filtering, monitoring, or human review. Tie each identified risk to a control that actually works in the deployed system.

None of this is set-and-forget: the guidance is to revisit risk and control decisions as models and deployments change. Future joint work will add more technical evaluation and mitigation guidance, with a public GitHub repository hosting evaluations, including community contributions.

What to do with this

Stop grading open-weight models by their license. For each deployment, document the use case, the data and tools in scope, and the users; name the risks using a shared taxonomy; attach an evaluation and a working control to each; and assign an owner. Then re-run that loop whenever the model or its deployment changes.
