Introducing Claude Haiku 5.5

The cheapest, fastest, and most capable small model Anthropic has released — built for high-volume work like summarization, classification, subagents, and browser use, and the first Haiku with an adjustable effort dial.

The idea in five lines

  • Cheapest and fastest Haiku yet — about 75% less to run on average than Haiku 4.5, and Anthropic's fastest model to date at standard speed.
  • An effort dial, a Haiku first — choose cost versus intelligence across Low to Max, so one model fits both throwaway and demanding tasks.
  • Built for high-volume work — summaries, compaction, classification, database queries, and as a subagent alongside Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5 on coding.
  • Big jumps on the benchmarks — OSWorld 2.1 72.4% (vs 15.7% for Haiku 4.5), GDPval-AA 1620 (vs 735), and HLE with tools 57.4% (vs 18.7%).
  • Available everywhere now — on the Claude API, AWS, Google Cloud, and Azure as claude-haiku-5-5; Sonnet 5.5 cache reads also got 50% cheaper.
So what for you

Audit your pipelines for work you send to a big model only because a small one wasn't good enough — summarization, classification, routing, subagent calls — and re-benchmark it on Haiku 5.5; at roughly a tenth of Sonnet's input price it can move real volume off your most expensive model.

What it's for

The small model that finally pencils out

Haiku 5.5 targets quick, repetitive, high-volume work — summaries, compaction, database queries, classification — and acts as a subagent next to Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5 on coding. Anthropic is clear about the boundary: the larger models are still better for complex agentic coding (for example, Terminal-Bench 4.0), while Haiku 5.5 suits narrower tasks that used to be cost-prohibitive.

Why it matters

Small-model economics decide what you automate

The price and speed of the cheapest model set the floor for what's worth doing at scale. A 75% cost cut plus an effort dial turns work that didn't pencil out — bulk summarization, context compaction, fleets of subagents — into default automation. The effort setting is the lever: dial it down for throwaway tasks, up for the ones that need the accuracy.
