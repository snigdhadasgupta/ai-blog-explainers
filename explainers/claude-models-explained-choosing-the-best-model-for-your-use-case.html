Claude Models Explained: Choosing the Best Model for Your Use Case

Five model classes, one recurring question: “which one should I use?” Anthropic’s default answer is counterintuitive — start with the most intelligent model, then dial cost down with effort level.

The idea in five lines

  • Default recommendation: start with the most intelligent generally-available model, then use effort level to tune performance and cost.
  • Cost-per-task is often lower for smarter models even when price-per-token is higher — they take fewer turns and less thinking to get it right.
  • Model classes don’t specialize by domain. Every Claude model is trained for coding, agents, and knowledge work; they differ in how hard a problem they carry, and at what price and speed.
  • Four questions decide it: how hard is the task, what are the latency needs, what are the access constraints, and what are the unit economics.
  • Benchmarks guide direction but saturate on frontier models — custom evals on your real workloads are how you actually choose.
So what for you

Choose your model by cost per finished task, not cost per token: start with the most capable model, then dial effort down until quality slips.

The default

Start smart, then dial down

The most frequent question Anthropic hears is “what model should I choose for this workload?” As more classes and versions shipped, the answer got more nuanced — but the starting move is simple: begin with the most intelligent model and use effort level to balance quality against cost.

Why start high? More capable models often take fewer turns and less thinking time to get most tasks right, so cost-per-task can be lower even when price-per-token is higher — especially at lower effort levels. Starting small also blurs the line between a model failure and a setup failure. As latency- or cost-sensitive cases arise, step down through the classes until you find your fit. (Some teams prefer the reverse: start cheapest, move up until the quality bar is met.)

Higher-class models at lower effort can be more efficient than smaller models — capability isn’t just about the ceiling, it’s about how few tries it takes.

The line-up

The Claude model family

One family, arranged by how hard a problem each class can reliably carry — not by domain.

Mythos / FableFrontier

Anthropic’s most capable class — coding, long-running agents, and problems AI hasn’t reliably solved before. Same underlying model in two packages: Mythos for trusted orgs doing dual-use cyber/bio work; Fable with added safeguards for general use. Both require limited data retention.

OpusPowerful

Reasoning-intensive enterprise tasks. Consistently ranks among leading models on benchmarks like GDPval-AA and Terminal-Bench 2.1. If Opus clears your bar, its speed and price may beat Fable; if it struggles, Fable’s extra wisdom and creativity is the answer.

SonnetVersatile

The everyday workhorse — a balance of performance, cost, and speed for the widest set of use cases, including high-volume sub-agents in multi-agent orchestration.

HaikuFastest

Lowest cost and fastest class, built for high-frequency workloads where latency and cost dominate.

How to decide

Four questions that pick the class

Model classes don’t specialize — there’s no “finance model” and “science model.” The difference is difficulty carried, at a cost in price and speed. Ask:

1

How hard is this task?

Lots of time, multiple steps, or previously unsolved → reach for a more capable class.

2

What are the latency needs?

High-frequency, customer-facing workloads → Sonnet is often the best choice.

3

What are the access constraints?

Mythos is limited to Project Glasswing orgs; not every class is available to every role.

4

What are the unit economics?

High production volumes may suit lower classes — if evals show the task is done satisfactorily. Tune with effort level.

The advisor strategy. A faster, cheaper worker model can call a more intelligent model to check its plan and grade its work. On SWE-bench Pro, Sonnet 5 with a Fable 5 advisor lands within 10% of Fable 5’s score at 63% of the cost of running Fable 5 for the whole task.
How to be sure

Benchmarks point; evals decide

Two ways to check whether a model is capable enough: standard benchmarks and custom evaluations. Benchmarks are helpful directional guides across classes and providers — but powerful models like Opus and Fable often solve nearly everything on a test (saturation), which stops telling you much.

When that happens, run the models on real workloads or your own evals: a curated set of problems drawn from production, including the hard tasks where your current tools fall short, with success criteria your team defines. This is where the capability and creativity of frontier models start to separate — from the pack and from one another.

There is no one-size-fits-all model. The best way to choose is to understand each class and understand your use case in depth — which means building, maintaining, and deploying strong evals.

Make the smart choice

Start with the most capable model, tune with effort level, and let evals on your real workloads settle which class ships to production.
