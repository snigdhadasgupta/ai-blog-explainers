AI Costs Like Electricity, Not Rent

AI breaks the old per-seat budgeting formula — the same tool can cost pennies for a summary or hundreds for a multi-agent task. Box’s guide to why AI spend is unpredictable, what drives it, and how to forecast it as a moving portfolio.

The idea in five lines

  • Cost scales with usage, not seats — consumption pricing bills tokens, API calls, compute hours, and vector queries, so the old “users × license fee” math no longer holds.
  • Rent versus electricity — traditional software is fixed like rent; AI is variable like electricity, where the bill depends on frequency, task complexity, and data volume.
  • Four variables drive spend — who uses it, what they do, how much content flows through, and when work shifts from human-triggered to autonomous agents.
  • Forecasts keep moving — rising model capability unlocks new use cases with no historical baseline, so a reasonable forecast can be obsolete in six months.
  • Optimize for value, not low tokens — consumption is a cost metric, value is an outcome metric; the goal is maximum value per unit consumed, not the smallest bill.
So what for you

Reframe the AI budget conversation from “how do we minimize spend?” to “how do we connect spend to measurable outcomes?” — model conservative, moderate, and optimized scenarios instead of a single number, and revisit them continuously as adoption and model prices shift.

Why AI breaks budgeting

No decades of licensing data to lean on

For decades, software budgeting meant counting users and multiplying by a license fee. AI breaks that formula, because the same tool can cost pennies for a quick summary or hundreds of dollars for a complex, multi-agent reasoning task. When a CFO asks “how much will this cost next quarter?”, the honest answer is “it depends on how people use it.” Underestimate and teams get blindsided by invoices; overestimate and leadership kills promising pilots.

Traditional software is like rent — a predictable cost tied to licenses. AI is like electricity — the unit price is published, but the total depends on how much you run.
The four cost drivers

What actually determines AI consumption

Figure 1 · Who, What, How much, When
1

Who — population

Active vs inactive users, power users vs occasional; adoption spreads unevenly across innovators, majority, and skeptics.

2

What — complexity

Summarization is cheap; classification, advanced reasoning, and agentic workflows cost far more per task.

3

How much — content

File count and size, document complexity (tables, handwriting, multi-column), update frequency, and seasonal peaks.

4

When — automation

Human-triggered work is consecutive; autonomous agents run concurrently, so consumption can spike as automation grows.

Why forecasts keep changing

Model like a portfolio, not a utility

Traditional forecasting assumes stable demand projected from history. AI resists that: as models get cheaper and more capable, new use cases emerge with no baseline — much like early electrification, where each new appliance created a new reason to consume power. Pilot-stage usage rarely predicts production, and per-token prices move too.

Utility-style

When it fits

A single, stable use case, well-understood usage, minimal automation, early maturity — a simple estimate works.

Portfolio-style

When you need scenarios

Multiple departments, expanding agentic or autonomous workflows, month-over-month change — build conservative, moderate, and optimized scenarios and rebalance.

Connect consumption to value

Cost is not the scoreboard

Too much of the AI conversation fixates on token counts, as if usage were the score. A household judges electricity by what it enables, not kilowatt-hours — and AI should be judged the same way, across economic, functional, experiential, and symbolic value. Consumption is a cost metric; value is an outcome metric.

Net AI Value = Business Outcomes − AI Consumption Cost. The goal isn’t to minimize consumption — it’s to maximize value per unit consumed.

Plan it in scenarios, then refine

Establish a baseline, model conservative/moderate/optimized versions, connect consumption to business outcomes, and review actual vs forecast continuously. Content governance — classifying, retiring, and retaining files — is itself a consumption lever.
