How JetBrains Vets a Frontier Model

JetBrains — maker of IntelliJ and Kotlin, serving 12.5M developers and 88 of the Fortune Global 100 — runs new models against its private monorepo before trusting them. CTO Vladislav Tankov on when Fable 5 earns the job and why safeguards are central.

The idea in five lines

  • Benchmark scores aren’t enough — JetBrains evaluates models on its own private repositories, because some models tune to public benchmarks but fall down on real work.
  • Cost per task beats cost per token — Fable 5 costs more per token, but its cost per task is lower on complex, long-running work, and it needed ~22% fewer steps than Opus 4.8.
  • A big jump on real code — Fable 5 posted a 44.3% Python pass rate vs 28.2% for Opus 4.8, solved 18 tasks Opus missed while losing only 2, and its passing code was more trustworthy.
  • Reach for it when you need a partner — Opus is the reliable workhorse; teams turn to Fable 5 for hard reasoning, near one-shots, and long-running agentic rewrites across frameworks.
  • Safety is a deployment discipline — JetBrains builds the safety net around the model and harness rather than tweaking the model, and accepts limited data retention as a fair trade for frontier access.
So what for you

Before you standardize on a model, build an eval on your own real workloads and measure cost per completed task, not per token — vendor benchmarks and sticker token prices both mislead on the work that actually matters to you.

How JetBrains decides

Trust the model on your own code, not the leaderboard

“We’re a coding company, so we have a big evaluation pipeline: large eval sets on private repositories, including our monorepo,” says CTO Vladislav Tankov. The team keeps separate leaderboards for best quality, best cost per task, and fastest model — and checks whether a model lives up to its benchmark scores on actual tasks, which a private repo makes far easier to verify.

Some models are tuned to score well on public benchmarks but fall down on actual tasks. With a private repository, that’s a lot easier to check.

When to use it

Opus is the workhorse; Fable 5 is the partner

“Opus is seen as a workhorse: you can be very sure it will do the work. You go to Fable 5 when you really need good reasoning, when you almost need a partner,” Tankov says — citing a tech lead who nearly one-shotted a rich text editor the team had attempted for years. Another use: long-running agentic experimentation where the agent generates specs from an existing app and rewrites it into another runtime or language in a near black-box setup.

Security is one of JetBrains’ biggest Fable 5 uses — running white-box testing against its own products to find vulnerabilities before outsiders run similar-class models to probe them.

Safety and data retention

Build the net around the model, not the model

JetBrains doesn’t try to make the safest model itself; it expects Anthropic’s red teaming to be sufficient and takes a systematic deployment approach — creating infrastructure and a safety net around the model and harness rather than tweaking the model. The balance is real: the less aggressive the classifier, the more vulnerabilities outsiders can find in JetBrains products.

On data, Tankov is candid: he’d prefer zero retention, but accepts limited retention for investigating serious flagged cases as “a fair tradeoff for access to frontier intelligence that allows my team to do their best work.”

What’s next: the developer cockpit

Tankov sees the frontier shifting from raw model capability to a “cockpit” for software development — a space where agents and people collaborate, non-technical roles take a larger role in software creation, and organizations get the governance and ROI clarity they need.
