From Green Dashboards to Real Exposure

A board doesn’t want to know how busy your security team is. It wants to know how exposed the company is — and whether that exposure is shrinking or growing. Box CISO Heather Ceylan’s fix: stop reporting KPIs to the board and start reporting KRIs.

Read at your level

The idea in five lines

  • KPIs measure your team — mean-time-to-remediate, patch-SLA attainment — and they stay mostly green while telling the board nothing about how exposed the company actually is.
  • KRIs measure the risk you’re carrying. The test for any indicator: does it track the exposure, or just the diligence of chasing it? If it’s the latter, it’s a KPI — send it back to the team.
  • Read each risk through three lenses: exposure (what’s open right now), remediation quality (do fixes hold), and prevention (do upstream gates stop issues as the company scales).
  • Score it so a red can’t hide: green/yellow/red worth 0/1/3 points, exposure weighted 1.5×, each risk normalized to its own ceiling into a 0–100% severity index you can trace to the indicators underneath.
  • The AI era is baked in: AI-assisted code inflates count-based metrics on its own, so indicators shift to rates and densities — and every risk, including agent and execution control, carries at least one AI-era indicator.
So what for you

Look at your next security or risk deck and ask of every number: does this tell me how exposed we are, or just how hard the team is working? The second kind belongs in a team review, not a board slide — and if your dashboard is all green while AI is multiplying your code and attack surface, the scale is probably measuring effort, not risk.

Why it matters

“Are we doing enough?” is the question KPIs can’t answer

Every board cybersecurity conversation comes back to three questions: what are our risks, what are we doing about them, and are we doing enough? The first two are easy. The third is hard, because it asks how exposed the company is — not how well the team performed.

A metric like “95% of criticals patched within SLA” looks reassuring and says nothing about the 5% still open: whether they’re internet-facing, or being actively exploited. Two forces make that gap dangerous. The window between a vulnerability being disclosed and weaponized has compressed, so “we made SLA” can coincide with days of live exploitability. And AI-assisted development ships code and configuration faster than teams grow, so count-based numbers inflate on their own.

The test for every indicator: does it measure the risk you’re carrying, or the diligence of chasing it? If it’s the latter, it’s a KPI — and it goes back to the team.

The framework

Three lenses on every enterprise risk

Box reads each of about ten enterprise risks — from vulnerability exposure and cloud misconfiguration to third-party compromise, unauthorized access, social engineering, and agent & execution control — through three lenses. Each risk carries at least one indicator aimed at AI-era exposure, built into the risk itself rather than bolted on as a separate “AI risk.”

Lens 1 · Now

Exposure

A point-in-time snapshot at quarter-close: what is open, reachable, or unprotected. This is the lens the board cares most about — and it should move the number the most.

Lens 2 · Lookback

Remediation quality

How fast issues close and whether the fixes hold. A fast fix that doesn’t hold just becomes recurring exposure, so velocity alone isn’t the measure.

Lens 3 · Upstream

Prevention

Do the controls that stop exposure from arising keep pace as the company scales? This is where the AI-volume problem is won or lost.

Example indicators

One indicator per lens, and why it’s different

Exposure

Time-weighted critical exposure

At quarter-close, multiply each open critical’s days-open by its current exploitation likelihood, then average. It rises when dangerous items sit and falls when weaponizable ones clear fast — moving independently of whether SLA was met. It replaces patch-SLA attainment.

Remediation

Recurrence rate

The share of this quarter’s closures that match something effectively closed before — same weakness class, same component. Low means durable fixes; high means you’re patching symptoms. No velocity metric would reveal this.

Prevention

Pre-merge catch rate

The share of defects caught by upstream gates before code merges, versus those that reach production. It counts outcomes, not scanners enabled — and it scales as code volume grows, AI-generated code included.

The scoring

A severity index a red can’t average away

The roll-up is deterministic: judgment goes into setting thresholds, and from there the same inputs always produce the same color. Each indicator scores green, yellow, or red — worth 0, 1, and 3 points. Red is deliberately more than proportional, because plain averaging would let several yellows outweigh one red, and a current exploitable exposure should never average away.

Figure 1 · How a raw score becomes a board color
1

Score & weight

Each indicator is 0 / 1 / 3 points. Exposure-lens indicators carry a 1.5× premium — current state should move the number more than lookbacks.

2

Normalize

Divide each risk’s weighted score by the maximum it could earn, so a risk with more indicators isn’t penalized. Result: a 0–100% severity index.

3

Band

Map the index onto a green / yellow / red scale. The example in the post: a risk at 67% is red because three indicators are red — not because a model guessed a loss.

Severity index = weighted indicator points ÷ maximum possible, banded to a single board-ready color.

Why not expected annual loss in dollars? It speaks the CFO’s language, but the moment a dollar figure appears, the conversation turns to challenging the estimate, the likelihood, the assumptions — time spent on the model instead of the exposure. A bounded, traceable index is shown to the board, but it opens the conversation rather than ending it: what leads is exposure, direction, and the actions on the reds.

The hard part & the lessons

The metrics are the easy part. The culture isn’t.

Security teams are trained to read a red as “something we control went wrong.” That’s true for a KPI — a red patch-SLA means the team missed. It is not true for exposure: a red exposure indicator can reflect a vendor-disclosed flaw, an actively exploited CVE on a system you don’t fully control, or a technique that outran a control you haven’t built yet. Teams first treat every red as a personal failure; over time they accept that a red exposure KRI is the program working as intended. Some of the most valuable indicators should sometimes be red.

Lesson 1

Let gray be a real answer

If a capability is still maturing and the data is missing, report gray — not a default green. A visible coverage gap beats hiding it under a reassuring color.

Lesson 2

Expect to baseline

Watch a number for a quarter or two before setting thresholds, so the threshold reflects your organization’s actual risk tolerance rather than a guess.

Lesson 3

Expect to keep tuning

All-red means thresholds are too low; all-green, too high. Either extreme means the scale isn’t discriminating — and thresholds are set with the business, not by security alone.

Where to start

Split your dashboard in two: KPIs for team reviews, KRIs for the board. Pick one exposure indicator, one remediation-quality indicator, and one prevention indicator per top risk, give each an AI-era version, and baseline for a quarter before you set thresholds. Budget about a quarter to build the indicators — and longer to teach the team that a red exposure KRI is not a verdict on their work.
